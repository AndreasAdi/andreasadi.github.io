---
title: "Let There Be: AI and the Oldest Temptation"
date: 2026-10-06
summary: AI is moving us toward a world where saying words makes things exist. That is the oldest picture of God we have, and the oldest temptation too.
---

The first thing God does in the Bible is speak. "And God said, 'Let there be
light,' and there was light" (Genesis 1:3). There are no tools in that sentence
and no materials, and nothing between the wanting and the having. The word is
enough.

The rest of Scripture keeps returning to it. "For he spoke, and it came to be;
he commanded, and it stood firm" (Psalm 33:9). John opens his gospel with "In
the beginning was the Word" (John 1:1), and Hebrews says the Son is "sustaining
all things by his powerful word" (Hebrews 1:3). For as long as people have tried
to picture what it means to be God, the picture has been this: you say it, and
it is.

I have been thinking about that picture a lot, because of what I do all day.

## From prompt to thing

For most of my life as a programmer my job was translation. Someone had an idea
in plain words, and I turned it into syntax a machine could follow. The distance
between "I want a page that shows my orders" and a page that shows orders was
weeks of work, and that distance was the whole profession.

That distance is collapsing. Today I describe a feature in a sentence and watch
it get written. You can describe a picture and get the picture, or a song, or a
short film. Agents take a paragraph and come back with a working app. Each year
the sentence gets shorter, the result gets bigger, and the human labour in the
middle gets thinner.

Follow the line forward. Language models already drive tools, tools already
drive machines, and machines already print, cut, assemble and build. Connect
them well enough and one day someone says "build a house here, three bedrooms,
lots of light," and a house begins to exist.

We are building, out of silicon, the oldest image of God we have.

## The oldest temptation

None of this is a new desire. It is the first one.

The serpent's offer in the garden was not money or pleasure. It was "you will be
like God" (Genesis 3:5). C.S. Lewis described what that lie did to us in *Mere
Christianity*:

> What Satan put into the heads of our remote ancestors was the idea that they
> could "be like gods" — could set up on their own as if they had created
> themselves — be their own masters — invent some sort of happiness for
> themselves outside God, apart from God. And out of that hopeless attempt has
> come nearly all that we call human history — money, poverty, ambition, war,
> prostitution, classes, empires, slavery — the long terrible story of man trying
> to find something other than God which will make him happy.

Isaiah gives the same ambition a voice: "I will ascend to the heavens … I will
make myself like the Most High" (Isaiah 14:13–14). And at Babel it becomes a
building project: "Come, let us build ourselves a city, with a tower that
reaches to the heavens, so that we may make a name for ourselves" (Genesis
11:4). God's verdict on Babel reads strangely in 2026: "If as one people
speaking the same language they have begun to do this, then nothing they plan
to do will be impossible for them" (Genesis 11:6).

One people, one language, nothing impossible. That is close to the pitch for a
general-purpose model trained on everything humans have written.

## Magic with a better interface

Lewis saw something else coming. In *The Abolition of Man* he argued that the
wise men of old asked how to bring the soul into line with reality, through
knowledge, self-discipline and virtue, while modern technique asks the opposite
question. "For magic and applied science alike the problem is how to subdue
reality to the wishes of men: the solution is a technique."

Speaking a word and having the world rearrange itself to match is the oldest
definition of magic there is. AI does not feel like engineering to most people
for a reason. It feels like a spell, and spells have always been a way of
getting what you want without submitting to anyone.

Lewis also warned about who ends up holding that power: "what we call Man's
power over Nature turns out to be a power exercised by some men over other men
with Nature as its instrument." When the words that make things are spoken into
a few companies' servers, it is fair to ask whose words they are.

## Naming, not creating

Look closely, though, and the imitation is easy to see.

When God speaks, "what is seen was not made out of what was visible" (Hebrews
11:3). That is creation from nothing. When I speak to a model, nothing like that
happens. My words go into a machine trained on billions of other people's words,
running on power from a power station, on chips made from sand somebody dug up.
Every "creation" rearranges things that already existed.

Lewis said this about human creativity in general, in a letter to Sister
Penelope: "We rearrange elements He has provided. There is not a vestige of real
creativity *de novo* in us." Try to imagine a new primary colour, he said, and
see what happens.

The Bible's first picture of human speech is the right size. God brings the
animals to Adam "to see what he would name them; and whatever the man called
each living creature, that was its name" (Genesis 2:19). God's word creates; our
word names. AI is an astonishing naming machine. It is not a creating one.

That does not make the desire to make things wicked. The first command given to
people was to make: "fill the earth and subdue it" (Genesis 1:28), given to
creatures made "in the image of God" (Genesis 1:27). When the tabernacle needed
building, God filled Bezalel "with the Spirit of God, with wisdom, with
understanding, with knowledge and with all kinds of skills" (Exodus 31:3).
Tolkien called this sub-creation: we make, he wrote, "by the law in which we're
made." A child who puts on his father's shoes is not rebelling. He is showing
whose child he is.

So technology is not outside God's claim. Abraham Kuyper put it in a line I
keep coming back to: "There is not a square inch in the whole domain of our
human existence over which Christ, who is Sovereign over all, does not cry:
'Mine!'" That includes the data centres. The question is never whether we
should make things. It is what, and for whom.

## Idols that talk back

Here is where it gets uncomfortable.

John Calvin wrote that "man's nature, so to speak, is a perpetual factory of
idols" (*Institutes* 1.11.8). We are always making something to trust in place
of God. For most of history the prophets could mock those idols for one obvious
weakness:

> But their idols are silver and gold, made by human hands. They have mouths,
> but cannot speak, eyes, but cannot see. … Those who make them will be like
> them, and so will all who trust in them. (Psalm 115:4–8)

Habakkuk sharpens it: "Of what value is an idol carved by a craftsman? Or an
image that teaches lies? For the one who makes it trusts in his own creation; he
makes idols that cannot speak. Woe to him who says to wood, 'Come to life!' …
Can it give guidance? It is covered with gold and silver; there is no breath in
it" (Habakkuk 2:18–19).

We have now built the first idols that can speak. They answer questions, give
guidance and comfort the lonely. Sometimes, exactly as Habakkuk says, they teach
lies with total confidence. And still there is no breath in them, not the breath
God breathed into Adam (Genesis 2:7). Psalm 115's warning has not expired: those
who make them will be like them. If I go to a machine for every answer, I should
not be surprised when I start to think like one.

John Piper put the difference well on his *Ask Pastor John* podcast: "No
machine, no computer, no AI will ever duplicate the spiritual reality of the
soul's enjoyment of God." A model can produce correct sentences about God. It
cannot love him. That is the line between a very good tool and a person.

## Every careless word

When the cost of making something drops to the cost of a sentence, the sentence
is all that is left. Skill stops mattering and intention becomes everything.

Jesus said that "the mouth speaks what the heart is full of" (Luke 6:45). For
most of history there was a lot of friction between the heart and the world. You
might want something cruel and never have the means, the time or the patience to
build it. That friction was a kind of mercy. Take it away and whatever is in us
comes out faster and at scale, the good and the rest.

Jesus also said: "everyone will have to give account on the day of judgment for
every empty word they have spoken" (Matthew 12:36). That verse used to sound
like it was about gossip. In a world where words become things, it sounds like
it is about everything.

## The Word who came down

What gives me peace is not that we will fail to build these things. We probably
will build them. It is that none of it is out of God's hands.

R.C. Sproul wrote in *Chosen by God*: "If there is one single molecule in this
universe running around loose, totally free of God's sovereignty, then we have
no guarantee that a single promise of God will ever be fulfilled." There is no
maverick molecule, and there is no maverick model. Whatever we build is still
built inside a world God holds together by his word. As Piper said in another
episode of the same podcast: "Our most advanced physics and artificial intelligence are a kindergarten
primer in God's library — at best."

And this is the real difference between the story we are telling with AI and the
story the Bible tells. At Babel people tried to climb up to heaven with their
own words and their own bricks, and it ended in confusion. In the gospel, God
does the reverse. He comes down. "The Word became flesh and made his dwelling
among us" (John 1:14). At Pentecost, the curse of Babel is turned around: people
from every nation hear about the wonders of God, each in their own language
(Acts 2). Not by climbing, but by grace.

We keep trying to become the speaker whose word makes the world. The good news
is that the only Speaker who can do that has already spoken to us, and what he
said was a person, not a product.

Augustine prayed at the start of his *Confessions*: "You have made us for
yourself, and our heart is restless until it rests in you." I think the race to
speak things into existence is that restlessness wearing a lab coat. We want the
power of the Word because we were made for the Word. The answer is not to stop
building. It is to remember who we are building in front of, to be careful what
we say, and to find our rest in the One who said "let there be" first.
